## Purpose

First principles thinking (reasoning from first principles) breaks complicated problems into their most fundamental truths and builds up from there. It helps you escape conventional wisdom, challenge inherited assumptions, and discover solutions that analogy-based thinking misses.

Use this when:
- Conventional approaches aren't working or feel limiting
- You need to innovate beyond incremental improvement
- Assumptions need explicit challenge ("we've always done it this way")
- Problem framing feels stuck or solution-space seems narrow
- Cost, feasibility, or "impossibility" claims need scrutiny

This is not a replacement for continuous improvement—it alters the *direction* of improvement. Without it, you optimize a bicycle; with it, you might build a snowmobile.

## Key Concepts

### What Are First Principles?

A first principle is a basic assumption that cannot be deduced further. Aristotle: "the first basis from which a thing is known." In practice, you don't need to go to the atomic level—going one or two levels deeper than most people often reveals new solutions.

### The Core Cycle: Deconstruct → Reconstruct

1. **Deconstruct:** Break the situation into its constituent parts. What are we absolutely sure is true? What has been proven? What are the raw materials, constraints, or invariants?
2. **Reconstruct:** Reassemble those parts in a more effective way. Different solutions present themselves at different layers of abstraction.

**John Boyd's snowmobile:** Motorboat (motor, hull, skis) + tank (treads, armor, gun) + bicycle (handlebars, wheels, gears, seat) → reassemble into a snowmobile. Unrelated fields, combined at the level of parts.

### Form vs Function

A major trap: optimizing *form* rather than *function*.

- **Form:** The current shape, structure, or convention (e.g., "a bag with straps," "a flying object that looks like a car")
- **Function:** The outcome you actually need (e.g., "store and move things efficiently," "transportation by flight")

**Suitcase example:** For millennia, bags and wheels existed separately. The rolling suitcase wasn't invented until 1970, when Bernard Sadow saw a worker rolling a machine on a wheeled skid. Innovators had been improving bags (form); Sadow improved moving things (function).

**Flying cars:** We have them—they're called airplanes. People fixated on form (car-shaped flying object) miss function (flight-based transport).

**Rule:** Optimize the function. Ignore the form. Project the *function* forward, not the form.

### Analogy vs First Principles

- **Reasoning by analogy:** "We'll do this because it's like something else" or "Nobody's done that, so it must not work." Mentally easier; limits possibility.
- **Reasoning from first principles:** Boil down to fundamentals, reason up from there. Harder; opens new paths.

Be wary of ideas you inherit. Old conventions, once accepted, set a boundary around creativity.

### Combine from Unrelated Fields

First principles often involves borrowing from seemingly unrelated domains. Gutenberg combined the screw press (wine-making) with movable type, paper, and ink to create the printing press. The best solution is not always where everyone is already looking.

## Techniques

### Socratic Questioning

A disciplined process to surface first principles and separate knowledge from assumption:

1. **Clarify thinking:** Why do I think this? What exactly do I think?
2. **Challenge assumptions:** How do I know this is true? What if I thought the opposite?
3. **Seek evidence:** How can I back this up? What are the sources?
4. **Consider alternatives:** What might others think? How do I know I'm correct?
5. **Examine consequences:** What if I'm wrong? What are the implications?
6. **Question the question:** Why did I think that? Was I correct? What conclusions can I draw?

### The Five Whys

Ask "why?" repeatedly until you hit irreducible truths. Children do this naturally. In practice, three to five "whys" often expose assumptions.

**Example:** "We need to add this feature." → Why? → "Because users asked for it." → Why do they want it? → "To accomplish X." → Why can't they accomplish X today? → "Because of Y." → Now you're at a level where first principles can apply.

## Application

Use `template.md` for a structured fill-in when guiding first principles analysis.

### Step 1: State the Goal or Problem

What are you trying to achieve? What is the *functional* outcome, not the assumed form?

### Step 2: List Assumptions and Inherited Beliefs

What have you been told is true? What "everyone knows"? What's "always been done this way"? Write these down explicitly.

### Step 3: Deconstruct to Fundamentals

- What are the constituent parts? (materials, constraints, invariants)
- What is *physically* or *logically* required? (laws of nature, math, causality)
- What would remain if we stripped away convention?

**Musk + rockets:** Rocket = aerospace aluminum, titanium, copper, carbon fiber. Commodity value of materials ≈ 2% of typical rocket price. The rest was convention.

**Musk + batteries:** Historical cost $600/kWh. First principles: cobalt, nickel, aluminum, carbon, polymers, steel can. London Metal Exchange spot prices → ~$80/kWh. The gap is manufacturing and combination, not materials.

### Step 4: Reconstruct from Fundamentals

Given those parts, what's possible? How could we combine them differently? What would a solution look like if we ignored "how it's always been done"?

### Step 5: Check Form vs Function

Are we still optimizing form? What's the *function* we need? Are we projecting form forward instead of function?

### Step 6: Apply Socratic or Five Whys When Stuck

If you're circling, use Socratic questioning or Five Whys to dig deeper. Often the block is an unchallenged assumption.

## Limiting Beliefs to Challenge

When you hear these, apply first principles:

- **"I don't have a good memory."** → How much can we physically store? Often more than assumed.
- **"There's too much information."** → What are the 3–5 variables that actually move the needle? Focus there.
- **"All the good ideas are taken."** → People have said this for centuries; new companies keep forming.
- **"We need to move first."** → iPhone wasn't first; it was better. First movers often fail.
- **"It's never been done before."** → Break it down. What's *actually* required? Plan for what could happen, not just what has.

## Common Pitfalls

### Pitfall 1: Stopping at One Level

**Symptom:** You list "assumptions" but they're still high-level conventions.

**Fix:** Go one more level. Ask "What are the parts of that?" or "Why would that be true?"

### Pitfall 2: Confusing Form with Function

**Symptom:** You're improving the current thing instead of the outcome.

**Fix:** Name the function explicitly. "What are we trying to accomplish?" Then ask if the current form is the only way.

### Pitfall 3: Reasoning by Analogy Without Noticing

**Symptom:** "We'll do X because Company Y did it" or "That won't work because nobody does it."

**Fix:** Ask "What are the first principles here?" Separate analogy from fundamental necessity.

### Pitfall 4: Over-Complicating

**Symptom:** Trying to reduce everything to physics or atoms.

**Fix:** You rarely need to go that deep. One or two levels deeper than the default view is usually enough.

### Pitfall 5: Dismissing First Principles as "Theoretical"

**Symptom:** "That's nice in theory, but we have to be practical."

**Fix:** First principles is practical. SpaceX, Tesla, CD Baby, BuzzFeed—all applied it to real businesses. The "practical" objection often masks unchallenged assumptions.
